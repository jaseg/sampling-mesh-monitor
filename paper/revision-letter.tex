\section*{Revision Letter: High Fidelity Security Mesh Monitoring using Low-Cost, Embedded Time Domain Reflectometry}
\vspace*{10mm}

We are grateful for the reviewers' thorough and insightful feedback during the 2025/4 review cycle. Below, we have
compiled a list of questions and comments by the reviewers. We will give detail how we addressed each point in the
present 2026/1 review cycle major revision and answer any questions.

Reviewer A stated,
\begin{displayquote}
    (...) I think the experiments could be expanded a bit to build more confidence (...)
\end{displayquote}

We have adressed Reviewer A's concern in the major revision by reworking all experiments, collecting a large amount of
additional data. In the new experimental section, each experiment has been repeated multiple times, and the section is
based on a total of about 1200 additional measurement series. Each measurement series consists of twelve traces and two
calibration traces of 768 points each.

Reviewer A asked,
\begin{displayquote}
    How sensitive is the proposed method to power fluctuations? More generally, what rate of false alarms should we
    expect? In a real-world scenario, there will be environmental impacts, and I'm unsure how robust the proposed method
    is.
\end{displayquote}

In the major revision, We have adressed Reviewer A's questions in two separate ways. First, we have conducted additional
experiments under a wide range of real-world environmental scenarios including inside a server room where
electromagnetic interference is abundant. These new results can now be found in Section 5.3.6 and Figures 14-16.
Furthermore, we have improved our experimental design based on Reviewer A's helpful input to more clearly separate the
experiment condition from concurrent environmental effects. To separate both effects in runs not focused on
environmental effects, we took alternating experiment and control measurements, ensuring that environmental effects
affect both groups equally. The experiments shown in Section 5.3.5 and Figure 11 where performance under
micro-soldering patching attacks is displayed benefitted the most from this improved experimental design.

Reviewer A asked,
\begin{displayquote}
2. Looking at the figures, I believe those are signals at the input of the ADC, not the output, correct? If so, how
likely is it to capture these tiny fluctuations with a microcontroller's ADC? Typically, these ADCs are not very fast or
precise. How fast and precise would the ADCs need to be to achieve good precision with your method?
\end{displayquote}

In addition to our clarifications in our 2025/4 cycle rebuttal response, we have addressed Reviewer A's questions by
adding Section 4.4 ``ADC Accuracy and Noise Immunity'' and by improving the text of Section 5.1.2
``Self-Characterization''. We additionally re-worded the beginning of Section 4 to more clearly explain the signal path
to the ADC.

Reviewer B noted,
\begin{displayquote}
Weaknesses
    \begin{itemize}
        \item lacking a more elaborated exposition of the detection method, e.g., algorithmically
        \item as a result, also no study on false positive/negative rates
    \end{itemize}
\end{displayquote}

Reviewer B further elaborated,
\begin{displayquote}
    Whereas the experimental results demonstrate 'visually' that the proposed circuit is able to distinguish several
    types of tampering attempts, it is not specified how the system will effectively detect tampering. E.g., which
    signal features will be extracted and how will these be processed algorithmically? Which thresholds will be set and
    how will they be calibrated? Etc. In other words, the 'back-end' of the tamper-detection solution is completely
    missing.

    As a result of this, it is not possible to fully assess the viability of the solution, since the most critical
    trade-off between sensitivity and stability cannot be assessed. In other words, how likely is it that a tampering
    event is effectively detected, and how likely is it that a non-tampering event (e.g., noise, or conventional
    handling) is mis-classified as tampering? A study of this in terms of false positive/negative rate needs to be
    performed.

    It is highly recommended to add an effective tamper detection algorithm to the solution, and study its false
    positive/negative rate under various scenarios.
\end{displayquote}

We agree with Reviewer B's observations and their insightful contextualization of the importance of such a detection
algorithm. To include such an algorithm, we reworked our entire experimental section from the ground up in the major
revision. We developed a baseline classifier to better illustrate our design's performance, and we performed a number of
new experiments to collect extensive data for the evaluation of our baseline classifier. We provide the design of our
classifier and detailed performance measurements throughout the reworked Section 5.3, now titled ``Classification
Performance''. As part of this rework, we replaced Figures 7-9 from the 2025/4 version with Figures 12 and 15 to improve
clarity and include new measurement data. Figures 7-11, 13, 14, and 16 are new in the revised paper and are based on the
new measurement data. To make space for the large amount of new work, we improved the wording of several parts of the
paper, and we condensed Section 4.1, ``Driver Selection'' and Section 4.2, ``Cost Breakdown'' to remove nonessential
detail.

During the evaluation of our new measurement data, we noticed PUF-like behavior of our system using our baseline
classifier as mentioned in Section 5.3.1. We believe a closer investigation of this behavior including an entropy
analysis and the construction of an optimized classifier would be an interesting topic for future work. We provide an
outline of this setting in the revised paper in Section 6, ``Future Work''. We expect that once this effect has been
characterized, it can serve as a basis for improved attack detection in a more advanced classifier.

Reviewer C noted,
\begin{displayquote}
    The authors are making all the code and the information available to the community. This is certainly a good
    contribution. Would have been interesting to have it already, in anonymous form, during the submission.
    Nevertheless, the fact that it will be available after is certainly to be appreciated.
\end{displayquote}

We fully agree with this evaluation and we have provided CAD data, source code, and raw data with the revised paper
during submission. We added anonymized source code and schematics as supplementary material to the major revision in
HotCRP. The raw data set was too large for direct upload in HotCRP, so we provided it as a (non-anonymous) download link
as a comment to the editors-in-chief.

In conclusion, we wish to thank the reviewers for their thorough and insightful reviews containing many helpful comments
and interesting questions. We hope we have provided clarity on all points raised, and we believe with the reviewers'
feedback we will be able to improve our revised paper further, creating a final version that is more understandable,
scientifically rigorous and well-founded than it would have been without the reviewers' helpful input.
